# USA residential VPS: What to check before you pay, with current LisaHost plans compared

A **USA residential VPS** is not simply a VPS located in an American data center. The part that changes the buying decision is the IP address: a residential or ISP-class IP is intended to resemble a connection assigned through a consumer broadband network rather than a conventional hosting ASN.

That distinction matters for people who need a persistent US network identity for remote work, regional testing, streaming, e-commerce operations, account management, or other legitimate workloads where a standard datacenter IP creates compatibility problems. It also explains why residential VPS products cost more than ordinary $5–$10 cloud instances.

## What “USA residential VPS” actually means

A normal VPS can have a perfectly legitimate US IP address while still being identified as a hosting or datacenter connection. A residential VPS changes the network identity rather than the basic virtualization concept.

For example, LisaHost's current US residential catalog describes its main 9929 and 4837 products as KVM VPS machines with one IPv4 address, NVMe storage, and dual-ISP home-broadband residential IPs. The New York and Chicago lines use the same general model, but their international routing is different from the mainland-optimized Los Angeles products.

There are three terms worth separating:

| Term | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| **US VPS** | The server is hosted in the United States. It says nothing by itself about IP classification. |
| **US native IP** | The IP is associated with the US rather than another country. It still does not automatically mean residential. |
| **US residential / ISP IP VPS** | The provider says the public IP belongs to an ISP/home-broadband class rather than a conventional hosting network. |

“Dual ISP” is another label you will see frequently in this market. It refers to how the IP is classified in network and IP databases. It is useful information, but it should not be treated as a universal guarantee that every website will classify an address identically.

That last point is important because independent discussions about LisaHost are not completely uniform. A 2026 IDC Flare discussion questioned whether every product marketed as “residential” should be treated as a conventional home broadband connection, arguing that an ISP-style ASN classification alone is not sufficient proof of physical residential access. That is a criticism of the classification claim, not proof that every LisaHost IP is unsuitable; it is simply a reason to verify the specific IP you receive before putting an important workflow on it.

## The first decision: residential VPS or residential proxy?

These products solve different problems.

A residential proxy is primarily about getting traffic through residential IP addresses, often with the ability to rotate among many addresses. A residential VPS is closer to renting a persistent computer with a stable public IP.

That difference makes a VPS more natural when you need:

* a fixed server environment;
* persistent software, files, browser profiles, or automation;
* administrator/root access;
* a stable outbound IP;
* a conventional VPS workflow with residential-style network identity.

It is less appropriate when your main requirement is switching through dozens or hundreds of IP addresses.

There is also a third option: a residential VDS or physically hosted residential machine. LisaHost lists those separately. Its US VDS catalog includes products described as being hosted in real residential homes and connected through local broadband providers. Those are **not mixed into the VPS table below**, because VDS is a separate product category with different infrastructure and refund conditions.

## 9929 vs. 4837: the real difference is the network profile

The Los Angeles 9929 and 4837 products overlap heavily on IP type and virtualization, but their advertised network characteristics are quite different.

The **9929 family** starts at ¥68/month and gives 50 Mbps with 1 TB of traffic. The standard tiers then move through 60 Mbps/2 TB, 80 Mbps/4 TB, and 100 Mbps/8 TB. Its unlimited plans deliberately trade bandwidth for uncapped traffic: 20 Mbps on Unlimited Lite and 50 Mbps on Unlimited Pro.

The **4837 family** starts at the same ¥68/month but gives 300 Mbps and 3 TB of traffic. The Advanced plan rises to 500 Mbps and 8 TB, while the Deluxe plan reaches 1 Gbps and 20 TB. Its unlimited tiers are also much more bandwidth-oriented than the 9929 unlimited products.

That makes the distinction fairly concrete:

> **9929 is the lower-bandwidth, lower-volume route family; 4837 is the higher-bandwidth, higher-volume family.**

You do not need to invent a theoretical “best” choice here. Just compare the actual workload to the limits.

A small fixed outbound workload may have little reason to pay for 1 Gbps. Conversely, moving large amounts of legitimate data can make the 300–1,000 Mbps 4837 tiers much easier to justify.

## What you should verify before buying

There are a few details worth checking on the checkout page rather than assuming from a generic “residential VPS” label.

### Check whether the IP is shared or dedicated

The main KVM residential VPS products in the table specify **1 IPv4 address**. The separate residential NAT product is different: it uses a shared IPv4 address with a limited number of TCP/UDP NAT ports. That NAT product is also manually provisioned and explicitly has no refund.

For a persistent server identity, that distinction is enormous.

### Check the traffic model

“Unlimited traffic” does not mean “unlimited bandwidth.”

LisaHost's 9929 Unlimited Lite, for example, is listed at 20 Mbps with unlimited traffic. The 4837 Unlimited Lite is 200 Mbps, while New York and Chicago Unlimited Lite are also 200 Mbps.

So ask two separate questions:

**How much data can I transfer?**

and

**How quickly can I transfer it?**

The answer can be completely different.

### Check the refund terms

The four main US residential VPS families advertise 48-hour refund coverage. Special residential products do not necessarily share those terms. LisaHost's US residential NAT page, for example, explicitly says there is no refund, while the residential VDS catalog uses a special “website balance only” refund condition.

That is another reason not to treat every “residential” product on the same site as interchangeable.

## The main buying mistake to avoid

The easiest mistake with a USA residential VPS is choosing based on the words “residential” and “USA” while ignoring everything else.

A much better checklist is:

* What exact IP classification do I need?
* Do I need Los Angeles, New York, or Chicago?
* Is 50 Mbps enough, or do I actually need 300 Mbps, 500 Mbps, or 1 Gbps?
* Do I need a fixed traffic allowance or unlimited traffic?
* Do I need Linux-style VPS administration or a Windows RDP desktop?
* Does my target application accept the IP I received?
* Does the provider's refund window actually apply to the product?
* Have I tested the IP before paying annually?
